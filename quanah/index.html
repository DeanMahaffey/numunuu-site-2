Quanah and the Star House

He led the last free Comanche band in to Fort Sill. Then he spent thirty-six years fighting for his people in a new way: with leases, lawyers, and a house so open to visitors that a president and hungry families alike came through the door. The ten stars on its roof are still there.

c. 1848Elk Creek, below the Wichita Mountains

A son of two families

Quanah was born around 1848. His own letter places his birth on Elk Creek, below the Wichita Mountains; other accounts put it farther west in Texas. His father was Peta Nocona, a Comanche leader. His mother was Nautda, born Cynthia Ann Parker, who had been taken as a girl of about nine in an 1836 raid on Fort Parker, Texas, and raised Comanche.

In December 1860 Texas Rangers attacked the family's camp on the Pease River and carried off his mother and his little sister, Prairie Flower. The Rangers said they had killed Peta Nocona. Comanche historians say he lived on and died years later of old wounds. Prairie Flower died in 1864. Quanah's mother died in 1871, never reunited with her Comanche family.

1874–1875Adobe Walls to Fort Sill

The last to come in

By his twenties Quanah was a war leader among the Quahadi, the band of the high Llano Estacado. On June 27, 1874, he helped lead the attack on the hide hunters at Adobe Walls and was wounded there. That fall the Army burned the camps in Palo Duro Canyon and killed the horse herds.

The Quahadi held out through the winter. In late May and early June 1875, Quanah led them in to Fort Sill. They were the last free band on the southern plains.

1875–1911The reservation years

A new kind of fight

The federal government named Quanah principal chief of the Comanche; his people never elected him to that office. He took his mother's surname and set out to win what could still be won.

He worked with Texas cattlemen he had once raided, including Charles Goodnight and the Burnett family, and pushed for the grazing leases that sent the tribes their first "grass money" in 1884. He became a wealthy rancher. He encouraged his people to farm and learn English, helped start a tribal school district, and went to Washington to lobby Congress.

He also kept what mattered to him. He kept his braids and his wives, and he was an early leader of the peyote religion that became the Native American Church, arguing for its legal right to practice. In 1902 he was elected a deputy sheriff in Lawton. He even appears on film, in the 1908 silent picture The Bank Robbery.

c. 1890On the reservation, near the Wichita Mountains

The house with stars on its roof

Quanah's cattlemen friends, Samuel Burk Burnett above all, paid for a two-story, ten-room house for him, at a cost of a little over $2,000. The government had refused to help. Ten white stars were painted on the roof.

Nobody knows for certain why. One story says the stars marked a rank equal to an Army general. Another, passed down and told by the house's present owner, says Quanah had stayed in a fine hotel in Washington and wanted the same at home.

The Star House was always full. Quanah and his wives fed hungry families and took in boys who had nowhere else to go. Guests included Geronimo, Charles Goodnight, and the Burnett and Waggoner ranching families. In April 1905 President Theodore Roosevelt came to visit and went wolf hunting nearby. Quanah had one of the first home telephones in Oklahoma, and a car.

1911February 23

Thousands came

Quanah died of heart failure at the Star House on February 23, 1911. Thousands of mourners came. He was buried at Post Oak Mission near Cache. In 1957 his remains were moved to the post cemetery at Fort Sill, to Chief's Knoll, beside his mother and his sister Prairie Flower.

TodayCache, Oklahoma

Saving the Star House

After Quanah's death his daughter bought the house. In the 1950s the Army's expanding artillery range at Fort Sill put it in danger of demolition, and it was moved off the post. It was later bought and moved again, to the grounds of an amusement park near Cache, where it still stands on private land.

The Star House was listed on the National Register of Historic Places on September 29, 1970. It has been named one of Oklahoma's most endangered historic places six times. A 2015 storm damaged it further. Restoration has been estimated at more than a million dollars.

As of Preservation Oklahoma's 2023 listing, the owners had reached an agreement to transfer the house to the nonprofit, with plans to restore it as a living museum and education center. It is not open to the public, and it sits on private property, so please don't go onto the land to see it.

Reading his story carefully

Quanah is one of the most written-about Comanche people in history, and much of what was written came from outsiders.

Dates disagree

Sources give his birth year anywhere from 1845 to 1852, and the count of his wives varies. We give the range where the record does.

"Last chief" is someone else's title

Newspapers and books called him the last chief of the Comanche. The office was created by the government, and after his death the Comanche moved to an elected chairman.

Family stories

Many Comanche families descend from Quanah and carry their own accounts of him. Those stories belong to them first. Corrections and additions are welcome.
