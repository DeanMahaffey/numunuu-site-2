Comanche history

Turning Points

Story 1

The horse

How a mountain people became the greatest horse riders on the plains.

Before 1700

People of the mountains

The Comanche were once part of the Shoshone, living in the high country to the north and west. Around 1700 their language began to separate from Shoshone, and they began moving south and east onto the plains.

1680

The Pueblo Revolt

In 1680 the Pueblo peoples of New Mexico rose up and drove the Spanish out of their country for more than a decade. Spanish horses scattered. Through trade and raiding, they spread north and east among the peoples of the plains.

1706

A new name

The earliest known written use of the name "Comanche" comes from 1706, when Spanish officials in New Mexico reported that Comanches were preparing to attack Pueblo settlements to the north.

Horses took time to spread. Spanish reports say that as late as 1725 Comanches were still using large dogs to carry their lodges.

1700s

Lords of the southern plains

Within a few generations the Comanche had become the finest horsemen on the continent. They pushed south onto the buffalo plains, drove the Apaches out of much of it, and built the Comanchería: a homeland stretching from the Arkansas River deep into Texas.

By the 1780s Spain was making peace with them on their terms. Read the 1786 Pecos treaty →

Story 2

The Great Raid of 1840

A peace council in San Antonio ended in killing. The answer rode all the way to the sea.

March 19, 1840

The Council House

Penateka leaders came to San Antonio to talk peace with the Republic of Texas. The Texans demanded the return of every captive held by the Comanche. When they did not get them all, they tried to take the chiefs prisoner. The chiefs resisted.

In the fighting that followed, the Texans killed the Comanche leaders who had come to make peace, along with many others in the delegation.

August 1840

Buffalo Hump rides

That summer the Penateka war leader Buffalo Hump led a great force of warriors, hundreds strong, from the hill country all the way to the Gulf of Mexico.

They struck Victoria, then Linnville, at the time the second-largest port in Texas. The town was plundered and burned. Its people escaped onto boats in the bay and watched it go up in smoke.

August 12, 1840

Plum Creek

On the way home, heavily loaded with horses, mules, and goods, the raiders were caught by Texas volunteers and Rangers at Plum Creek, near present-day Lockhart. It became a running fight across the prairie.

The raiders lost most of what they had taken. Comanche losses are disputed: Texans claimed dozens killed, but far fewer bodies were found.

After

A broken trust

The raid showed that Comanche power could reach the sea. It also left the Penateka and the Texans with bitter memories on both sides. It took Sam Houston years of patient talks to bring them back to a treaty council.

Story 3

Adobe Walls, 1874

The buffalo were disappearing. The people tried to stop it.

1867–1874

The hide hunters

The Medicine Lodge treaty of 1867 promised that the Comanche could hunt south of the Arkansas River for as long as the buffalo lasted. Within a few years, commercial hide hunters were killing the southern herd by the hundreds of thousands, for leather sold back east.

In 1874 hunters and merchants set up a trading post at Adobe Walls in the Texas Panhandle, deep in Comanche hunting country. Read the Medicine Lodge treaty →

June 27, 1874

The attack

At dawn, an estimated 700 Comanche, Cheyenne, Kiowa, and Arapaho warriors attacked the post. They were led by Quanah, a young Quahadi war leader, and Isatai, a medicine man who had promised them protection from the hunters' bullets.

Inside were 28 men with heavy, long-range buffalo rifles. The attack failed. As many as 70 warriors may have been killed, though estimates vary, and Quanah was wounded.

Summer 1874

The Red River War

Isatai's promise had failed, and it was a hard spiritual blow. For the Army, the attack was the reason it needed. Columns of soldiers marched into the Panhandle from several directions to hunt down every band still living free.

The war that followed would end life on the open plains.

Story 4

Palo Duro Canyon

The safest place on the plains, until it wasn't.

Fall 1874

The canyon refuge

As the soldiers closed in, Comanche, Kiowa, and Cheyenne families gathered in the great red canyon of Palo Duro, below the rim of the Llano Estacado. There was water, grass, timber, and shelter, and they had laid in food for the winter.

September 28, 1874

Dawn attack

Colonel Ranald Mackenzie's Fourth Cavalry found the camps and came down a narrow trail into the canyon at first light. Most of the people escaped up the canyon. Accounts of how many were killed differ widely.

The soldiers burned the lodges, the robes, and the winter food in great bonfires.

The next day

The horses

The soldiers drove the captured horse herd to Tule Canyon. Mackenzie kept some for his scouts and ordered the rest shot: more than a thousand horses, and by some counts closer to two thousand.

Without horses, without lodges, and without food, the families could not survive the winter on the plains. By November, many had walked in to the reservation.

Story 5

Coming in, 1875

The last free band, and what came after.

Spring 1875

The Quahadi

Through the winter, one band held out on the Llano Estacado: the Quahadi, with Quanah among their leaders. By spring the buffalo were going fast, and the Army would never stop coming.

In late May and early June 1875, Quanah led the Quahadi in to Fort Sill. The free life on the southern plains was over.

1875–1911

A new kind of fight

On the reservation Quanah became a rancher and businessman, a leader who dealt with Washington and with Texas cattlemen, and he lived in a large house near Cache, Oklahoma, called the Star House. He died in 1911.

In 1892 the Jerome Agreement broke up the reservation into allotments, and in 1901 the rest was opened to settlers. Read the Jerome Agreement →

Today

Still here

Seventy years after Palo Duro, Comanche men carried their language onto Utah Beach as code talkers. Their story →

The Comanche Nation today is headquartered in Lawton, Oklahoma, with about 17,000 enrolled citizens. The people, and the language, are still here.
