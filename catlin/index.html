Catlin, Ta-wa-que-nah, or the Rocky Mountain, Near the Comanche Village, 1834–1835. Smithsonian American Art Museum.

Online exhibit · Paintings by George Catlin

Painted in 1834

In July 1834, a column of United States dragoons rode into Comanche country, and a painter from Pennsylvania rode with them. Sick with fever, he stayed near a Comanche village for weeks and painted what he saw: the lodges, the families, the chiefs, and the horsemen. These are among the first paintings of Comanche people made from life.

How he got there

A peace mission, a fever, and a sketchbook

George Catlin had already traveled up the Missouri River in 1832, painting the nations he met there. In 1834 he got permission to travel with the First Dragoon Regiment, sent out from Fort Gibson to meet the Comanche, Kiowa, and Wichita and open talks with the United States.

The summer was brutal. Fever moved through the column. Catlin later estimated that as many as 150 men died. When the dragoons pushed on toward the Wichita village, Catlin was too sick to ride, and he was left behind with other sick men near the Comanche camp.

So he painted. Portraits were made in camp. Many scenes were sketched there and finished later in his studio. Sixteen paintings, on four walls below, show the camp, the horsemen, and the people he met. Tap any painting to see it large.

  1. June 1834The dragoons leave Fort Gibson, in what is now eastern Oklahoma. Catlin rides along as a civilian.
  2. July 14Comanche riders meet the column under a white flag and lead the soldiers to their village.
  3. Mid-JulySick men, Catlin among them, are left near the Comanche camp. The rest march west through the Wichita Mountains.
  4. July 21–22Council at the Wichita village, joined by Comanche and Kiowa leaders. Gen. Henry Leavenworth dies the same week.
  5. AugustThe survivors straggle back to Fort Gibson.
  6. August 1835The talks lead to the Treaty of Camp Holmes, the first treaty between the United States and the Comanche. See it in the treaties exhibit.

Reading Catlin carefully

These paintings are a rare record, and they are also one outsider's view, made in a few weeks, alongside an army. Here is what to keep in mind.

The names are his spellings

Catlin wrote names the way they sounded to him, then gave his own English translation. They are not modern Comanche spellings, and we have not changed them. If your family knows these names another way, we would like to hear it.

The words are of his time

Catlin admired the people he painted and said so often. He also used the language and ideas of an American in 1834. Where we quote him, the words are his, not ours.

Some scenes were finished later

The portraits were painted in camp. Many of the larger scenes were sketched there and finished in his studio over the next few years, so details may be arranged for the picture.

Only a few weeks

Catlin saw one village, in one summer, while he was sick. The people in these paintings had names, families, and histories far beyond what he wrote down.

See them in person

In 1852, deep in debt, Catlin lost his whole Indian Gallery to Joseph Harrison Jr. of Philadelphia, who had lent him money against it. Harrison's widow gave the collection to the Smithsonian in 1879.

Smithsonian American Art Museum

Washington, D.C. Most of the Comanche paintings are kept in storage. At our last check, Bow and Quiver was on view in the Luce Foundation Center, third floor. Every painting here links to its museum record.
