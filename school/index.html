Fort Sill Indian School

For 109 years, Native children were sent to live and learn at a boarding school a few miles from where the Comanche National Museum stands today. Some remember it with pride and lifelong friendships. Others remember punishment for speaking their own language. Both are true.

A note before you read. This history is still close for many families. If it brings up hard feelings, the National Native American Boarding School Healing Coalition has resources for survivors and descendants.

1869–1875The Quaker years

A school on the reservation

In 1869 a Quaker farmer named Lawrie Tatum became the federal agent for the Kiowa, Comanche, and Apache. He founded a school at the agency, and it opened in 1871 with 24 students. Josiah and Elizabeth Butler, also Quakers, were its first teachers.

At first, Comanche and Kiowa families refused to send their children. In 1874 the agent set up a school board with Native members, and in 1875 twenty Kiowa children enrolled, a third of the school. From then on the school was often more crowded than it was built for.

1891–1950sThe Indian Service years

Trades, chores, and English only

In 1891 the school became nonsectarian, no longer run as a Quaker school. Around 1900 it returned to its original campus and took the name Fort Sill Indian School. It grew from one building to thirty.

The purpose was to turn Native children into Americans on the government's terms. Into the 1950s, boys trained mostly in farming and trades, and girls in homemaking. Former students describe strict discipline and harsh punishment, including punishment for speaking their own languages.

Sickness was a constant danger in crowded dormitories. In 1911 an outbreak of trachoma, an eye infection that can cause blindness, reached nearly 80 percent of the school.

Some of the men who had been discouraged from speaking Comanche at government schools later carried that same language into World War II as code talkers. Charles Chibitty was a Fort Sill Indian School student. The Code Talkers →

1904Building 309

The stone dormitory

In 1904 a stone girls' dormitory was built in just seven months. A fire badly damaged it in 1907, and it was rebuilt. Later came a stone bath annex, a brick addition in 1930, and a wooden sleeping porch.

The building was emptied in 1964 and ordered torn down. The National Park Service objected, and it was saved. In 1973 it was listed on the National Register of Historic Places. It is the building in the photo at the top of this page.

1945–1980From many nations

A school worth fighting for

Before World War II, most students came from western Oklahoma: Comanche, Kiowa, Apache, Caddo, Delaware, and Wichita children. After the war the school took in Navajo students from New Mexico and Arizona, who within a few years made up about 80 percent of the school. In 1960 the farm program ended and classes turned more toward academics. By 1970 most of the roughly 300 students were again from Oklahoma.

When the Bureau of Indian Affairs announced it would close the school in the late 1970s, students, staff, alumni, and tribal leaders fought back, and kept it open a few more years. For many of them it had become their own school. It closed for good in 1980.

Since 1980What remains

Holding on to the place

In 1982 the Kiowa, Comanche, and Apache tribes raised $200,000 to turn the old dormitory into an archive center. The project did not succeed, and in 2000 Preservation Oklahoma named Building 309 one of the state's most endangered historic places.

In 2024 the KCA Intertribal Land Use Committee pulled the school's old stone entrance sign out of a creek near Lawton, where it had been lost for decades after a truck knocked it down in 1958. It read "U.S. Indian Service, Kiowa Indian Hospital, Ft. Sill Indian School." The committee planned to clean it and return it to the campus.

The bigger storyFederal Indian boarding schools

One school of hundreds

Fort Sill was one of hundreds of boarding schools the United States ran or supported for Native children. In July 2024 the Department of the Interior finished its investigation of that system. It counted 417 federal Indian boarding schools across 37 states or territories, and at least 973 Native children who died in them between 1819 and 1969. The report says the true number is greater.
