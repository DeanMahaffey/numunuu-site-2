An exhibit · 1860–1875

Winter as a Weapon

The Army could rarely catch Comanche riders in summer. So it learned to fight something else: the winter itself, and the food, lodges and horses families needed to survive it.

A note before you read. This exhibit is about attacks on Comanche families, including women and children. It describes what happened plainly, without graphic detail. These were someone's grandparents.

Two mornings

December 25, 1868

Christmas morning

Across much of the United States it was a holiday: church bells, family tables, a day of rest.

It is still how most Americans picture that date.

The same morning

North Fork of the Red River

Below the Quartz Mountains, in what is now southwestern Oklahoma, a Comanche village was in its winter camp. It was snowing.

Soldiers were coming up the river with two mountain howitzers.

The attack on that village was no accident of timing. It was part of a plan.To see why, start with the seasons.

Why winter

Life on the southern plains followed the seasons. Pick a season to see what changed, for Comanche families and for the soldiers hunting them.

Summer

"Their ponies would be thin, and weak from lack of food . . . their movements would be so much impeded that the troops could overtake them."

General Philip Sheridan, on why he planned his campaign for winter. Personal Memoirs, vol. 2.

A simplified picture. Life varied from band to band and year to year.

Four winters

Scroll, and the map follows the story.

Winter 1

Pease River, 1860

Before it was a strategy, it was a lesson.

December 19, 1860

A camp on the Pease

About sixty Texas Rangers and militia under Captain Sul Ross found a small Nokoni Comanche camp on the Pease River, in what is now Foard County, Texas.

Most of the people in the camp were women. Nearly everyone there was killed.

Taken

Nautda and Prairie Flower

The Rangers carried off a woman and her baby daughter. She was Nautda, born Cynthia Ann Parker, taken from Texas as a girl of about nine and raised Comanche. She was Quanah's mother.

Prairie Flower died in 1864. Nautda died a few years later, never reunited with her Comanche family.

Winter 2

The winter campaign, 1868

A general turns the season into a plan.

November 1868

Columns in the snow

General Philip Sheridan sent three columns of soldiers into the southern plains as winter came on. One marched from Camp Supply in Indian Territory, one from Fort Lyon in Colorado, and one, under Major Andrew Evans, east from Fort Bascom in New Mexico.

The outer columns were meant to drive the bands toward the center, like hunters beating game toward a line of guns.

November 27, 1868

The Washita

At dawn, Lieutenant Colonel George Custer attacked the Cheyenne village of Black Kettle on the Washita River. His orders included killing the horses, and soldiers shot hundreds of them.

It was a Cheyenne village, not a Comanche one. But it showed every nation on the southern plains what the winter campaign meant.

December 25, 1868

Soldier Spring

On a snowy Christmas morning, Evans's column found a Comanche village on the North Fork of the Red River. Two howitzers fired into the lodges.

Warriors held the soldiers off so the women and children could get away. Kiowa warriors from a village downstream rode in to help. The families escaped toward Fort Cobb or west into the Texas Panhandle.

The soldiers burned the village and the winter meat supply. The Army estimated twenty to twenty-five warriors were killed. The Comanche acknowledged one death: a leader named Arrow Point.

January 8, 1869

A fort at Medicine Bluff

Two weeks later, Sheridan staked out a new post below the Wichita Mountains, in the heart of Comanche country. It became Fort Sill.

With no lodges and no winter food, many families had little choice but to come in to the agencies that winter.

Winter 3

Palo Duro, 1874

Not a winter attack. An attack on winter.

Fall 1874

Ready for winter

During the Red River War, Comanche, Kiowa and Cheyenne families gathered in Palo Duro Canyon, below the Llano Estacado. There was water, grass and shelter, and they had laid in their food for the winter.

September 28, 1874

Everything burned

Colonel Ranald Mackenzie's cavalry came down into the canyon at dawn. Most of the people escaped up the canyon on foot.

The soldiers burned more than 450 lodges and the stored winter meat. The next day, at Tule Canyon, they shot the captured horses: 1,048 by Mackenzie's own count, and by other counts many more.

Accounts of how many people died range from three, in Mackenzie's report, to dozens. But killing people was not the main goal. Winter was coming.

Winter 4

The last winter, 1874–75

Winter finishes what battles could not.

Winter 1874–75

Walking in

Without horses, lodges or food, band after band could not last the cold months on the plains. By November 1874 many had walked in to Fort Sill. Others came through the winter.

Late May and June 1875

The Quahadi

One band held out through that winter on the Llano Estacado: the Quahadi, with Quanah among their leaders. With the buffalo nearly gone and the soldiers never stopping, they came in to Fort Sill in late May and early June 1875.

They were the last free band on the southern plains.

How people survived

This is not only a story of what was done to Comanche families. It is also a story of what they did.

Covering the retreat

At Soldier Spring, warriors held the soldiers back so the women and children could escape the village.

Neighbors came

Kiowa warriors from a village downstream rode to the Comanches' aid on Christmas Day, slowing the soldiers' advance.

Scattering

At Palo Duro, most of the people got out of the canyon alive. Families scattered across the plains and found each other again.

Holding out

The Quahadi lived through the winter of 1874 to 1875 on the Llano Estacado, with almost nothing, and chose when to come in.

Whose version?

Most of what we know comes from the men who carried out these attacks. Their accounts don't always hold up.

Sheridan's memoir, on Soldier Spring

He called the village outlaws from the main tribe and wrote that most of the women and children were captured.

Later history

The Oklahoma Historical Society's account says the warriors held the line so the women and children could escape, and they fled toward Fort Cobb or the Panhandle.

Sul Ross, on Pease River

Ross reported that his men killed Peta Nocona, Quanah's father, in the fight. His own telling of it changed over the years.

Quanah

Quanah always said his father was not at the camp, and lived on for years before dying of old wounds. Later historians have backed him.

December 25, today

The campaigns ended life on the open plains. They did not end the people.

Today the Comanche Nation is headquartered in Lawton, Oklahoma, a few miles from where Sheridan staked out his fort, with about 17,000 enrolled citizens. This December 25, Comanche families will spend the day as they choose, in their own homes, through their own winter.
