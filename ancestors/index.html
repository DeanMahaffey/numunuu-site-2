A free guide

Finding Comanche Ancestors

Your family's story is written down somewhere: on census rolls, allotment lists, and agency papers kept for more than a century. Here is how to find them, one step at a time.

Start the steps
Before you begin. This is a research guide, not legal or enrollment advice. Only the Comanche Nation decides who can enroll. This site is independent and not connected to the Nation's Enrollment Department.

Six steps back in time

Work from today backward. Every generation you confirm makes the next one easier to find.

  1. Start at home

    What your family already knows

    Before any archive, talk to your relatives. Older family members often hold the names, places and dates that unlock everything else. Write down every version of a name you hear.

    • Names: English names, Comanche names, nicknames, and maiden names. Clerks wrote Comanche names many different ways, so keep every spelling.
    • Places: where people lived, went to school, worshipped and were buried. Many Comanche families lived around Lawton and the towns of Comanche County.
    • Papers: birth and death certificates, obituaries, funeral programs, family Bibles, old letters, school records, military discharge papers.
    • Numbers: an allotment number or census roll number written on old papers is gold. Copy it exactly.
    Tip

    Record elders on your phone, with their permission. A voice telling a story is a record too.

  2. The last hundred years

    Recent records

    Use certificates and obituaries to reach the generation born around 1900 to 1940. Obituaries often name parents, brothers and sisters, and where someone was born.

    • Obituaries: the Lawton Public Library's family history room keeps a compiled set of Comanche, Kiowa and Apache obituaries.
    • The federal census: taken every ten years. The census is a good place to find American Indian ancestors' names alongside their neighbors.
    • Vital records: births, marriages and deaths. The Kiowa Agency kept its own vital records for Kiowa, Comanche, Apache and Fort Sill Apache people, and some from 1924 to 1937 are on FamilySearch.

  3. 1885 to 1940

    The Indian Census Rolls

    Each year, federal agents counted the people enrolled at their agency. Comanche people were counted at the Kiowa Agency, later run from Anadarko. These rolls are one of the best ways to follow a family year by year.

    What a roll showsIndian and English names, sex, age, relationship to the head of the family, and roll numbers
    From 1929Allotment or annuity numbers, for tribes that had them
    WhereNational Archives microfilm M595. Indexed with images on FamilySearch

    You'll need a name to start. Search the person, then look at the whole page: families are listed together, so a single entry can show you parents, children and grandparents.

    Good to know
    • Not every year survives for every tribe.
    • Ages drift from year to year. Look for the same family across several rolls.
    • The rolls list people who kept a formal tie to the agency, so a relative who moved away may drop off.

  4. About 1901

    The allotment rolls

    This is the most important record for Comanche families. After the Jerome Agreement, Congress passed the Act of June 6, 1900, and Comanche people received individual allotments of land around 1901. The rest of the reservation was opened to settlers.

    The people who received those allotments form the base roll of the Comanche Nation today. If you can connect your family to an allottee, you have found the anchor of your Comanche line.

    National ArchivesAllotments, about 1901: microfilm 7RA249, rolls 1 to 7, at the National Archives in Fort Worth
    Lawton libraryA schedule of land allotments and allotment plat maps for southwest Oklahoma

  5. The deep files

    Kiowa Agency records

    The agency that served the Kiowa, Comanche and Apache kept far more than censuses. These files can answer questions the rolls can't: who inherited land, who married whom, who went to which school.

    • Births, marriages, divorces, deaths and wills, 1869 to 1925.
    • Estate records, 1872 to 1925, which can name the heirs in a family.
    • School enrollment records. Children's school records often turn up in agency files, including for schools far from home. See Fort Sill Indian School.
    • Payments: per capita and annuity payment lists.

    The Oklahoma Historical Society holds the Kiowa Agency records on microfilm (series KA, 101 rolls), free to view at its Research Center in Oklahoma City. Staff can't search names for you, so bring what you've found.

  6. If you want to enroll

    Comanche Nation enrollment

    Membership is set by the Comanche Nation's constitution, Article III. In short:

    • The base roll is everyone who received an allotment as a Comanche under the Act of June 6, 1900, and later acts.
    • Descendants of allottees with one-eighth (1/8) or more degree of Comanche blood are eligible.
    • Proof means authenticated copies of birth certificates or other records recognized by state or federal recorders.

Common mix-ups

A few things that send people down the wrong road.

"Look for them on the Dawes Rolls."

Not for Comanches. The Dawes Rolls cover the Five Tribes: Cherokee, Creek, Choctaw, Chickasaw and Seminole. Comanche families are found on the Kiowa Agency census rolls and the 1901 allotment rolls.

"A DNA test will show my tribe."

Membership is by documented descent. The Comanche Nation enrolls descendants of allottees, proven with records like birth certificates. A DNA test can't stand in for that paper trail.

"The name has to match exactly."

Spellings wander. Clerks wrote Comanche names by ear, and the same person can appear several ways. Search by family, age and place, not one spelling. You can see this on the treaty signer lists.

"Great-grandma was a Comanche princess."

A family story is a start, not proof. Many families carry stories like this. Treat it as a clue, and follow the records to see where it leads.
