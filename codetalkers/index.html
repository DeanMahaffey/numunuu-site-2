1940–41Oklahoma

A language the Army needed

As war spread across Europe, the U.S. Army looked for a way to send battlefield messages that no enemy listener could understand. A language almost no one outside one nation spoke was the answer.

Seventeen young Comanche men from Comanche communities in Oklahoma joined up. Charles Chibitty enlisted on January 2, 1941, and trained at Fort Benning, Georgia. In April 1941, Second Lieutenant Hugh Foster was put in charge of the Comanche group and the work of building a code began.

Some of these men had gone to government boarding schools, where speaking Native languages was often discouraged. Now the same language was going to war on the Army's side.

1941–43Building the code

Turtle means tank

Comanche had no words for tanks, bombers, or machine guns, so the men made them. They chose Comanche words for things that looked or acted like the new machines. Altogether they built about 250 terms for military and geographic words.

For names and words that weren't in the code, they spelled letter by letter, with a Comanche word standing for each letter. Saddi, dog, stood for D.

Even another Comanche speaker couldn't follow a message without knowing the code. Tap a card to turn it over.

Comanche spellings are shown only where the Army's own published accounts give them. Spellings in English letters vary from record to record.

June 6, 1944Utah Beach, Normandy

The first message

The Comanche code talkers served with the 4th Infantry Division. Two were assigned to each of the division's three regiments and three to division headquarters, so that every part of the division could pass messages in Comanche.

On D-Day, thirteen of them came ashore at Utah Beach. Charles Chibitty landed with the 22nd Infantry Regiment. One of the first messages he sent back:

“Five miles to the right of the designated area and five miles inland, the fighting is fierce and we need help.”

Charles Chibitty, Utah Beach, as he later recalled it

Fourteen Comanche code talkers served in Europe, in Normandy, the Battle of the Bulge, and other battles of the war.

1989–2013Recognition

Honored, late

For decades their work was barely known. In 1989 the government of France honored the Comanche code talkers. On December 3, 1999, Charles Chibitty was honored at the Pentagon in the Hall of Heroes.

Chibitty died on July 20, 2005, the last surviving Comanche code talker. On November 20, 2013, the Comanche code talkers were awarded the Congressional Gold Medal, alongside code talkers from many other Native nations.

“What we did, we saved lives, using the Native American language.”

Charles Chibitty
